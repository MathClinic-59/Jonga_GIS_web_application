Part 1 — Assessing models with alternative data¶

Q1. Data understanding¶

Sagaceta-Mejía et al. (2024, pp. 3–5) collect daily Open, High, Low, Close, adjusted Close, and Volume from Yahoo Finance for ECH, EWZ, and IVV. From these market observations they derive technical features such as moving averages, momentum, rate of change, relative strength index (RSI), and volatility statistics. Technical indicators transform noisy raw price histories into interpretable summaries of trend, momentum, and variability. They are predictors rather than guaranteed causes of future prices; their apparent usefulness must be tested on observations not used to fit the model.

Worked calculations in Markdown¶

Simple moving average of length $k$: $SMA_k(t)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}P_{t-j}$.

One-day return: $r_t=\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}-1$.

Price momentum: $M_k(t)=P_t-P_{t-k}$.

Rolling volatility: $\sigma_k(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(r_{t-j}-\bar r)^2}$.

Why classification rather than regression?¶

The article's target describes direction, not exact future price size: $\Gamma(t)=1$ if $Open(t)-Open(t-1)>0$, and $-1$ otherwise (Sagaceta-Mejía et al. 2024, p. 3). Direction classification supports a simple up/down decision and avoids requiring the neural network to estimate a precise continuous price change.

Two other possible classifications are (i) three-class direction, $-1$ for returns below $-1\%$, $0$ for returns between $-1\%$ and $1\%$, and $1$ above $1\%$; and (ii) volatility regime, $1$ when realized five-day volatility exceeds its training-sample median and $0$ otherwise. These are our alternative proposals, not definitions used in the paper.

Forecasting caution: The paper's contemporaneous $\Gamma(t)$ is not necessarily a genuinely forward-looking label. For a defensible trading prediction we use next-session opening-price direction, $y_t=\mathbb{1}[Open_{t+1}>Open_t]$, with features known at the end of day $t$. This is a clearly stated methodological adaptation.

Descriptive statistics vs. models: Pearson correlation measures pairwise linear association and does not train a multivariate prediction model. LASSO fits a penalized model whose coefficients are shrunk toward zero; a neural network learns a nonlinear predictive mapping. The authors select informative indicators, combine ranked feature subsets, and test MLP accuracy to reduce the number of inputs and training cost (Sagaceta-Mejía et al. 2024, pp. 5–9).

LASSO objective: $\min_{\beta_0,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_i(y_i-\beta_0-\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol{\beta})^2+\lambda\sum_j|\beta_j|$. A larger $\lambda$ encourages sparsity.

Q4. Understanding features¶

A feature is a numeric input column such as RSI-14, 10-day momentum, or SMA ratio. A method is an operation such as Pearson correlation, LASSO, or tree-based ranking that assesses or selects features. A model (e.g., MLP) maps selected inputs to predictions. Feature categories include price-level and trend, returns and momentum, volatility, volume/liquidity, and transformations of market information. The paper considers a broader technical-indicator catalogue; this notebook implements a small transparent subset. Selecting a small, stable set can reduce noise, computation, and overfitting, but must be judged on held-out data (Sagaceta-Mejía et al. 2024, pp. 4–8).

Q5. Optimization, cross-validation, and distances¶

Cross-validation repeats model evaluation on different training/validation subsets. In $k$-fold CV, the sample is split into $k$ folds; each fold serves once as validation, while the remaining $k-1$ folds train the model. The article uses $k=10$ with shuffled folds (Sagaceta-Mejía et al. 2024, p. 7). Shuffling time series can overstate trading usefulness, so a chronological validation is also shown below.

Jaccard distance for sets of selected features $A,B$: $$d_J(A,B)=1-\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}=\frac{|A\triangle B|}{|A\cup B|}.$$ If $A=\{SMA,RSI,MOM\}$ and $B=\{RSI,MOM,VOL\}$, intersection size is 2, union size is 4, so $d_J=1-2/4=0.5$.

Euclidean distance $d_2(x,y)=\sqrt{\sum_j(x_j-y_j)^2}$ and Manhattan distance $d_1(x,y)=\sum_j|x_j-y_j|$ compare numerical vectors, whereas Jaccard compares membership in sets. The article calls a selected feature set useful when it provides high cross-validated classification accuracy with fewer features and lower training cost; there is no guarantee of a globally optimal trading strategy (Sagaceta-Mejía et al. 2024, pp. 7–9).

Step 1. Financial problem¶

The paper addresses the challenge of selecting a small set of indicators that classifies ETF market direction while avoiding the high computational cost of feeding many technical indicators into an MLP. This matters for market timing and risk management, although predictive accuracy alone does not establish a profitable trading rule. Emerging-market ETFs may have different volatility, concentration, liquidity, and country-specific exposures from developed-market funds; this motivates checking feature stability separately for EWZ, ECH, and IVV rather than assuming a universal indicator set (Sagaceta-Mejía et al. 2024, pp. 3–4, 8–10).

Step 2. Application and paper findings¶

The study reports that selecting a small fraction of its indicator universe improves classification accuracy by approximately 2 percentage points in its principal comparison; with an additional early-stopping experiment, it reports an average 13.63% accuracy improvement and 84.68% training-time reduction under that specific setup (Sagaceta-Mejía et al. 2024, abstract and p. 8). These figures are reported findings, not results from this notebook. The paper's selected indicators differ by ETF; no single feature should be assumed universally dominant. Our replication evaluates common trend, momentum, RSI, volatility, and volume indicators, but does not claim to recreate the exact selected features in the paper's Table 5.

Step 3. Partial replication — technical indicators and target¶

All features below are computed from historical data at or before date $t$. We use Open[t+1] > Open[t] as the label. After calculating rolling features, we align predictors and labels by the same date index before computing Pearson correlations. This avoids the incompatible-dimensions problem observed in the earlier R Markdown version.

Pearson correlation: $$r_{xy}=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\sqrt{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}}.$$ Ranking is computed only on each training fold; doing it before CV would leak validation information.

Feature selection and model validation¶

For each fold, calculate $|r_j|=|corr(X_j,y)|$ on training observations only. Retain the top five features. Standardize inputs within the training fold, then train a small MLP. Report fold accuracy, balanced accuracy, and selected features. We also show a majority-class baseline. Random 10-fold CV follows the paper's broad approach; expanding-window TimeSeriesSplit is a separate robustness analysis, not the same experiment.

def rank_corr(Xtr,ytr):
    aligned=Xtr.copy(); aligned['_target_']=np.asarray(ytr)
    scores=aligned[feature_cols].corrwith(aligned['_target_']).abs().fillna(0).sort_values(ascending=False)
    return scores

def model():
    return Pipeline([('scale',StandardScaler()),('mlp',MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(16,),max_iter=300,early_stopping=True,n_iter_no_change=12,random_state=SEED))])

def evaluate(cv,name):
    records=[];counts={f:0 for f in feature_cols}
    for fold,(tr,te) in enumerate(cv.split(X,y),1):
        xtr,xte=X.iloc[tr],X.iloc[te];ytr,yte=y.iloc[tr],y.iloc[te]
        selected=rank_corr(xtr,ytr).head(5).index.tolist()
        for f in selected:counts[f]+=1
        for kind,cols in [('Selected five',selected),('All features',feature_cols)]:
            clf=model();clf.fit(xtr[cols],ytr);pred=clf.predict(xte[cols])
            records.append({'CV':name,'fold':fold,'model':kind,'accuracy':accuracy_score(yte,pred),'balanced_accuracy':balanced_accuracy_score(yte,pred),'n_features':len(cols),'features':', '.join(selected) if kind=='Selected five' else 'all'})
        base=np.repeat(ytr.mode().iloc[0],len(yte))
        records.append({'CV':name,'fold':fold,'model':'Majority baseline','accuracy':accuracy_score(yte,base),'balanced_accuracy':balanced_accuracy_score(yte,base),'n_features':0,'features':'none'})
    return pd.DataFrame(records),pd.Series(counts).sort_values(ascending=False)

Part 2 — Satellite data as an alternative financial data source¶

Required for groups of three only. This section is an approximately five-page user-guide draft to expand/format in the separate PDF report. Sun et al. (2024, p. 7, Table 1) classify satellite data under sensing data, describing commercial data as relatively costly and often low-publicity; public earth-observation programs also exist and should be distinguished from commercial vendors.

1. Sources of data¶

  • Copernicus Sentinel-2: multispectral optical imagery (Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem).
  • USGS Landsat: historical multispectral imagery (USGS EarthExplorer / Earthdata access).
  • NASA VIIRS night-time lights: proxy for some dimensions of human activity.
  • Commercial providers: higher-resolution or specialized data products, generally subject to licensing and cost.

The research review discusses satellite imagery for economic activity, agricultural output, and business/financial prediction (Sun et al. 2024, pp. 7, 16–17). Satellite signals are proxies, not direct observations of corporate sales or stock returns.

2. Data types and structure¶

Typical formats: GeoTIFF rasters (pixel grids with a CRS), STAC/JSON metadata, cloud masks, vector AOI boundaries (GeoJSON), and tabular aggregates (CSV/Parquet). Important fields include timestamp, location/geometry, band value, pixel size, quality flag, and processing level. Time stamps and coordinate reference systems must be standardized before merging with financial data.

3. Quality and validation¶

Check cloud cover, shadows, revisit interval, sensor calibration, missing scenes, atmospheric correction, spatial resolution, land-cover change, sampling bias, temporal alignment, and look-ahead leakage. Compare remotely sensed proxies against independently observed activity where possible; do not treat night-time light intensity as a direct accounting measure.

4. Ethical and legal considerations¶

Respect licensing, terms of use, security restrictions, and spatial privacy. Even when individual persons are not identifiable in a public image, repeated fine-scale monitoring can create privacy and fairness concerns. Avoid selective use of inaccessible commercial information without discussing unequal investor access and possible information asymmetry (Sun et al. 2024, pp. 16–17).

7. Short research guide and further reading¶

Sun et al. (2024, pp. 7, 16–17) review how satellite imagery is used for stock-return prediction, commodity production/pricing, and regional economic-performance measurement. The paper references satellite applications including Subash et al. (2018); consult the review's reference list for full bibliographic details before adding that work to the final submission. A robust literature extension should search for peer-reviewed studies of night-time lights and economic activity, crop yield estimation using multispectral imagery, and parking-lot/retail activity proxies, recording DOI, geography, study period, and validation method for each study.

Practical workflow: obtain permission and imagery → define AOI and time interval → mask cloud-contaminated pixels → aggregate features at an appropriate spatial scale → merge with financial outcomes using publication/availability dates → validate on a held-out later period → discuss uncertainty, licensing, and economic interpretation.

Limitation: This user guide illustrates a reproducible schema and EDA but does not claim to have ingested a genuine satellite scene. Replace the synthetic demonstration with a sourced real dataset if the assignment evaluator requires empirical sample data.
